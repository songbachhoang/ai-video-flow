How I think about the role

It's a function to own, not a queue of clips

The instinct most people bring is “make good videos.” Mine is to ask: what's the marginal cost and time of the next on-brand video, and how do I drive both toward zero while quality holds? That reframes the job from a creative seat into an operations-and-product seat — which is what owning a workflow actually is.

It also maps cleanly onto an AI-first, inventory-to-demand business. If the company already coordinates stock and demand across departments, then a flow that can turn any SKU — including the stock you need to move — into on-brand video on demand is the marketing arm of that same loop. I'm not interviewing to be “the video person.” I'm interviewing to own that arm.

✗ The asset mindset

Brief in, video out. Quality lives in one person's taste. Cost and turnaround are whatever they are. Doesn't survive a 400-SKU catalogue.

✓ The system mindset (mine)

A repeatable pipeline with measured unit economics. Taste is captured once and reused. Cost-per-usable and yield are tracked and improved every cycle. Scales to the whole catalogue.

How I work

The principles I'd run the flow by

These aren't theory — each one is something I either applied or learned the hard way during the two-hour build below.

1

Systems over assets

I optimise the cost of the next video, not the polish of one. The repeatable pipeline — version-controlled, one-command — is the real deliverable; the clip is its output.

In practice · I shipped the flow as a repo (pipeline.md, prompts.md, logs, a make-campaign command) before I shipped a “final” video.

2

Measure before you spend

I preflight cost and report cost-per-usable and yield — never vibes. Numbers are how a workflow earns trust and budget.

In practice · Measured real cost at $2.10/clip — my own first estimate was ~4× too low. I quote what I measured.

3

Taste at the UI, scale in code

Dial the look by hand until it's locked, then codify it and batch. The first unit is artisanal; the Nth is automated. That's how quality scales without a person in every loop.

In practice · Locked one character + kit + grade, then reused them across every shot via references.

4

Consistency is an input problem

Brand and identity consistency is won in the reference pack — a reusable anchor — not by re-prompting and hoping. One anchor serves the whole catalogue.

In practice · One original character held across a 4-shot story with zero drift.

5

One gate, one stop-loss

Quality is decided at a single QA gate against a rubric; a re-roll cap protects margin. Discipline is what keeps an AI pipeline from quietly burning money.

In practice · 5-dimension rubric + a 3-re-roll cap; the final cut needed zero re-rolls.

6

Story sells; spectacle doesn't

A pretty clip is dead on arrival. The job is the narrative and the voice — what makes a viewer feel something and share it. I'll kill my own output the moment it's just b-roll.

In practice · My first pass was handsome and hollow. I scrapped it and rebuilt it as a scripted, voiced story.

7

Own the guardrails

Owning the pipeline means owning the risk: likeness rights, brand safety, and a human gate before anything goes live. I'd rather state the line than learn it in a lawsuit.

In practice · Declined to generate a real celebrity's likeness — even when asked — and kept publishing a human decision.

8

Trust nothing unmeasured

Tools lie by omission. I verify what the platform tells me against ground truth before I plan a budget or a roadmap around it.

In practice · The billing API hid a pricing tier; I caught it against the real UI before committing spend.

How I'd build it

One flow, eight stages, a loop that compounds

A pipeline anyone on the team could re-run for the next SKU — “run a campaign for X” becomes a single command. UI for taste, code for scale.

1 Brief→ 2 Reference pack→ 3 Shot list→ 4 Generate→ 5 QA gate→ 6 Assemble→ 7 Human publish→ 8 Measure ↺

The loop is the point: every winning prompt and reference pack is promoted into a template library, so each cycle is cheaper and better than the last. And because the platform aggregates many models, I treat models as swappable inputs behind the flow — the system survives model churn, it doesn't depend on any one of them.

Thinking long-term

From one clip to a defensible function

How I'd grow this from a proof into a moat — each phase compounding the last.

Phase 1Prove the unit✓ done in 2 hrs
One SKU → one hero, with real unit economics. Establish the pipeline, the QA rubric, and the cost/yield baseline. (Done — see the proof below.)
Phase 2Catalogue scale
Reusable anchors per product line; batch campaigns from one command; a metrics layer tracking yield, $/usable and turnaround — then downstream watch-through and CTR. The flow now runs for any SKU on demand.
Phase 3Close the inventory→demand loop
The cross-departmental play: slow-movers and deadstock auto-trigger on-brand video; a virality signal gates spend before publish; performance feeds back into the template library. Marketing becomes a function of inventory and demand signals, not a separate silo.
Phase 4The function as a moat
A proprietary template + QA library and a cost curve competitors can't match. The org spins a campaign for any SKU, any channel, at a known cost-per-usable. That's the defensible asset — and the reason this is a function to own, not a task to outsource.
