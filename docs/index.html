A note on the role · for Crossian

How I'd run your video engine.

What the job actually is, what I'd do in the first ninety days, and the longer game. I built a working version of it in an afternoon — the proof's at the bottom.

Bach Hoang  ·  June 2026

Most people read “AI video” as a creative brief. I read it as an operations problem wearing a creative costume. The question that matters isn't can we make a good video — anyone with a decent eye and a model subscription can make one. It's: what does the second hundred cost, how long do they take, and do they still look like us? Fix that and video stops being a bottleneck and becomes a tap you turn on, one SKU at a time.

Crossian is interesting to me for a specific reason: you're already wiring inventory to demand across departments. Video is one missing edge of that graph — if the system knows a SKU needs to move, the same system should be able to commission the video that moves it, on brand, at a unit cost you can put in a spreadsheet. So that's where I'd start.

But let me be clear about what I'm offering, because it isn't a pair of hands for video. It's a way of working: take an expensive, manual, judgment-heavy function, turn it into a measured AI system, and make it run without me. Video is the first place I'd prove it — a clean, measurable bottleneck. It won't be the last.

The first ninety days

What I'd actually do, if you hand me the keys

Week 1 · Baseline the truth. Stand the pipeline up as code — I already have a working version — and run one real SKU end to end while measuring the things nobody measures: cost per usable clip, yield (usable ÷ generated), and brief-to-export time. You can't manage what you're guessing at. On my own test the real cost came in at four times my first estimate. I'd rather find that on day one than in a board deck.

By day 30 · A repeatable unit. One product line with a locked reference pack — a reusable identity/character anchor, the brand grade, a do-not-show list — so any SKU in that line is shot from a template, not from scratch. Plus a QA rubric anyone can apply, so quality isn't trapped in my head. The bar: a campaign for a new SKU shippable in under a day, by someone who isn't me.

By day 60 · Plug into the loop. Wire the flow to your inventory and demand signals so the SKUs that need video get queued automatically — starting with deadstock and slow movers, where the upside is obvious and the creative bar is forgiving. Add a pre-publish gate (quality, brand, rights) and an engagement read before we spend a dollar on distribution.

By day 90 · Prove the curve. A dashboard the rest of the company can read: cost-per-usable trending down, yield trending up, turnaround shrinking, and the first downstream numbers — watch-through, click-through, sell-through on the SKUs we filmed. The pitch to leadership isn't “look at these videos.” It's “here's the cost curve, and here's where it's headed.”

The longer game

Where it compounds into a moat

Past the first quarter, three things stack on top of each other.

The library becomes the moat. Every prompt, reference pack, and shot template that works gets promoted into a shared library. The 400th video is cheaper and more on-brand than the first because it inherits everything that worked before it. That library, plus a cost curve a competitor can't match, is the defensible asset — and the reason this belongs in-house, not at an agency that rebuilds from zero every brief.

Build vs. buy, decided on purpose. I'd rent the models and own the orchestration. Models change monthly; betting the function on any single one is a mistake, so I keep the flow model-agnostic and swap the best tool per shot. What we build is the part that accrues value to us — the libraries, the QA, the inventory hooks. Rent the commodity, own the compounding bit.

It becomes a team, not a hero — and that frees me to move. The whole reason to write this down as a system is that it stops depending on me. Once the flow runs without me, the company has a durable asset and I'm free to point the same playbook at the next expensive, manual function. That's the arc I'm actually after: deliver this, make it self-running, then go do it again somewhere it matters more. I'm not here to be the video person for five years — I'm here to be the person who turns the org's bottlenecks into AI systems, one after another.

How I work

A few things I believe

All of these I either used or learned the hard way building the test below.

  • Measure before you spend. I preflight cost and quote cost-per-usable and yield — not adjectives.
  • Consistency is an input problem. You win it in the reference pack, not by re-prompting and praying.
  • Story over spectacle. My first cut was handsome and hollow b-roll. I scrapped it and rebuilt it with a script and a voiceover — a workflow owner has to be willing to kill their own output.
  • Own the guardrails. I declined to generate a real person's likeness even when it would've been easier. Owning a pipeline means owning its risks, not just its outputs.
  • Trust nothing unmeasured. The platform's own billing API hid a pricing tier from me; I caught it against the real UI before planning a budget around it.
